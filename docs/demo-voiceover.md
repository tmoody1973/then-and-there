## 1. The problem, and the three ways in

[warm, measured] The Library of Congress has scanned twenty-three point eight million pages of American newspapers. Almost nobody reads them: search only works if you already know what to type. In 1898, a Black weekly in Wisconsin called an Appleton barber, J. E. Ellmore, "the smartest gentleman of our race." Without his name, you would never find it.

Then and There opens three ways in: daily games, shelves that need no name, and an agent that watches new pages for your family name or hometown.

## 2. How Meta's models do an editor's job

Behind it, Meta's models do an editor's job. Muse Spark reads the page image and boxes every story, ad and headline. SAM three point one traces each piece. Muse Spark picks out the human stories, and re-reads the scan where the archive's text is garbled. Then a second check, the Editor, compares every clue and claim against the scan. If it can't confirm something, readers never see it. So far: six hundred and seven pages from one hundred forty-one papers, about four cents a page, with seventeen percent of drafts rejected.

## 3. First way in: the stories come to you

The first way in: the stories come to you. In the Detective case, players follow Nellie Bly's 1889 race around the world, using only what the papers had printed by then. In One Day, it's August 26th, 1920: gray is the wire story every paper shared, and red is what each paper wrote itself. And in Spot the Forgery, Meta's Muse Image changes one thing on a real page, always labeled as altered.

## 4. Second way in: no name needed

The second way in doesn't need a name at all. The Story Finder opens on shelves instead of a blank search box: the Black press, German Milwaukee, the race in the papers. Every story sits on its scan, says whether the Editor verified it, and cites itself.

## 5. Third way in: an agent for your name

The third way in is for when you do have a name. Every night, the agent reads every page the Library of Congress just added. The matching happens in Then and There's own code; a reader's list never goes to an AI model. To show it at work, this demo replays a batch the Library added on October 23rd: one thousand seven hundred and six pages of the Gainesville Daily Sun, watching for one name known to be inside. It found the name, and the Editor checked the scan to confirm it names a person. Then it left this note, labeled Replay. July 1st, 1909. [slowly] "Miss Nora Mullin left yesterday for a long visit with her sister in Tampa. Her smiling face will be much missed at the post office window." [measured] And on November 1st, it found a real match in pages from Guam that the Library had published days before.
